# Notes: This American Life #436, "The Psychopath Test" (May 27, 2011)

Episode page: https://www.thisamericanlife.org/436/the-psychopath-test
Transcript: https://www.thisamericanlife.org/436/transcript

*Our own notes, with fictitious names for private people and radio staff (key: `docs/09-about-page-story.md`). We do not copy the transcript.*

## Prologue (about 6 min)
The staff take a shortened psychopathy test from a forensic psychologist ("Dr. Barnes"). He trusts their answers instead of checking records, as he would in a real case. Much of the test turns out to be about childhood and teenage behavior. Staff bet on Rosa, June or the host, Ivan.

## Act One, "Underachievement Test" (about 28 min)
- A California lifer ("Marcus Hale"), convicted as an accessory to a 1985 murder, is described by family and his mentor as transformed after decades in prison.
- California gives lifers the PCL-R before parole hearings. His high score was a major reason he was denied.
- A defense psychologist ("Dr. Ortiz") says the test leans on historical factors that can't change.
- Robert Hare's history: lab studies of low fear response in psychopaths; the 1980 checklist (20 traits, 0/1/2 each, max 40, ~30 cut-off); a follow-up study showing high scorers reoffended far more; early reluctance to give the test to the justice system; official publication in 1991.
- Hare today: ambivalent; worried about poorly trained users.
- A UVA researcher (Daniel Murrie) found opposing experts' scores differed by about 8 points on average, with prosecution experts scoring higher.

## Act Two, "King of the Forest" (about 15 min)
Jon Ronson (author of *The Psychopath Test*) visits a retired CEO known for mass layoffs ("the Lion King CEO"). The CEO reframes trait after trait as leadership. Ronson later realizes he wanted to find a psychopath and discounted evidence that didn't fit. His rough estimate puts the CEO high but below the clinical line.

## Act Three, "The Results Are In" (about 6 min)
Every staff member scored zero. Dr. Barnes joked they were too neurotic to be psychopaths; their regret over past misdeeds showed empathy.

## Lessons the agents should carry
1. Expert scores can lean toward whoever is paying (Villain Panel).
2. Checklists invite confirmation bias (Spin Doctor, Ethics reviewer).
3. History-heavy scoring can't see change (Then vs. Now).
4. Real assessment needs a clinician, an interview and records; ours is entertainment (everyone).
