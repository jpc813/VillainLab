# Notes: Murrie, Boccaccini, Turner, Meeks, Woods & Tussey (2009)

**"Rater (dis)agreement on risk assessment measures in sexually violent predator proceedings: Evidence of adversarial allegiance in forensic evaluation?"** *Psychology, Public Policy, and Law*, 15, 19–53.
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014897

*Our own notes; the article is linked, not copied.*

## Question
Risk tools like the PCL-R show strong agreement between raters in research studies. Is agreement just as strong when the raters are hired by **opposing sides** in real court cases?

## Design
- Reviewed real sexually-violent-predator civil commitment cases where evaluators on opposite sides each reported scores for the same offender.
- Instruments included the STATIC-99, the MnSOST-R and the PCL-R.

## Findings (summary)
- Agreement between opposing experts was **much weaker** than in research settings.
- Differences tended to lean in the direction that favored the side that retained the expert.
- *This American Life* #436 describes this line of work: about an 8-point average gap on the 40-point PCL-R, with some cases differing by 10 to 20 points, and prosecution experts scoring higher.

## Limits the authors and later work point out
- Field data can't prove the hiring caused the gap; attorneys may choose sympathetic experts. The 2013 experiment was designed to test that (see `murrie-2013-adversarial-allegiance.md`).

## Why it matters for the Villain Lab
- It shows the gap isn't just a lab curiosity; it appears in real, high-stakes cases.
- The Villain Lab's typical 12–22 point gaps on a 100-point index are in a similar spirit, scaled for a game.
