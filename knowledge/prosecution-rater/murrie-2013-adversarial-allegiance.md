## Question
Can forensic experts stay objective when one side of an adversarial case is paying them? Earlier field data showed opposing experts disagreeing, but that could just mean lawyers pick experts who already agree with them. This study tested whether the hiring side itself causes the bias.

## Design
- **108 forensic psychologists and psychiatrists** were recruited and paid for what they believed was a real consultation for a state agency reviewing sexually-violent-offender case files.
- They met an actual attorney. Some were led to believe they were consulting for the **prosecution**, others for the **defense**.
- Unknown to them, everyone scored the **same four case files** on two widely used, well-researched risk-assessment instruments (one of which was the PCL-R).

## Findings
- Experts who thought they worked for the prosecution gave **higher risk scores**; those who thought they worked for the defense gave **lower** scores to the same offenders.
- The effect was moderate to large (reported effect sizes ranged up to about d = 0.85).
- The authors call this **adversarial allegiance** and describe it as strong evidence that the effect exists for at least some experts.
- Murrie said the team was surprised how easy the effect was to find, and that most expert witnesses believe they are objective.

## Why it matters for the Villain Lab
- It is the scientific basis of the Prosecution Rater vs. Defense Rater design.
- Raters should lean toward their side the way real experts did: by **emphasizing and interpreting** evidence, not by inventing it.
- The Judge's job mirrors what the study asks of the legal system: spot the lean and correct for it.
